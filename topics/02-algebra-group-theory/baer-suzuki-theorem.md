# Baer-Suzuki Theorem

> **Topic:** Algebra & Group Theory · **ID:** `02-algebra-group-theory/baer-suzuki-theorem` · **Status:** solved-recently

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

The classical Baer-Suzuki theorem provides a local, element-wise criterion for identifying the maximal normal $p$-subgroup of a finite group. Let $G$ be a finite group, let $p$ be a prime, and let $x \in G$ be a $p$-element (an element whose order is a power of $p$). The theorem states that if for all $g \in G$, the subgroup generated by $x$ and its conjugate $x^g$, denoted $\langle x, x^g \rangle$, is a finite $p$-group, then $x$ is contained in $O_p(G)$, the $p$-core of $G$. Equivalently, the normal closure $\langle x^G \rangle$ is a $p$-group. 

While the classical theorem is a settled fact for finite groups, the broader "Baer-Suzuki Problem" encompasses a family of quantitative, probabilistic, and structural generalizations: 
1. **Quantitative form:** Is it sufficient to check only a bounded number of conjugates (e.g., $k$ conjugates) to guarantee membership in $O_p(G)$?
2. **Infinite and algebraic formulations:** Under what finiteness conditions does the theorem extend to profinite groups, algebraic groups, or general locally finite groups?
3. **Probabilistic form:** If $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is a $p$-group for a "large enough" proportion of elements $g \in G$, must $x \in O_p(G)$?

Recent breakthroughs have resolved the quantitative and probabilistic forms for finite simple groups and continuous limits, classifying this expanded scope as recently solved.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

The problem operates at the intersection of finite group theory, local analysis, and probabilistic group theory. 

- **$p$-Elements and $p$-Groups:** A group $H$ is a $p$-group if the order of every element is a power of $p$. By Lagrange's Theorem, a finite group is a $p$-group if and only if its order is $p^k$ for some integer $k \ge 1$. An element $x \in G$ is a $p$-element if its order is $p^m$.
- **The $p$-Core $O_p(G)$:** For a finite group $G$, $O_p(G)$ is the unique largest normal $p$-subgroup of $G$. It is the intersection of all Sylow $p$-subgroups of $G$:
  $$O_p(G) = \bigcap_{P \in \text{Syl}_p(G)} P$$
- **Normal Closure:** For an element $x \in G$, its normal closure $\langle x^G \rangle$ is the smallest normal subgroup of $G$ containing $x$, generated by all its conjugates:
  $$\langle x^G \rangle = \langle g^{-1} x g \mid g \in G \rangle$$
- **The Baer-Suzuki Criterion:** The classical mathematical statement requires that for all $g \in G$, the group:
  $$H_g = \langle x, g^{-1} x g \rangle$$
  satisfies $|H_g| = p^a$ for some $a \in \mathbb{N}$.

In the context of algebraic groups over a field $K$ of characteristic $p$, $O_p(G)$ is analogous to the unipotent radical $R_u(G)$, and the theorem examines unipotent elements.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

The theorem was originally conjectured and proved for primes $p \ge 5$ by Reinhold Baer in 1957. Michio Suzuki completed the classical proof for $p = 2$ in 1961, utilizing his profound work on CA-groups and CN-groups. These original proofs relied heavily on character theory and early classification results.

A major historical milestone occurred in 1995 when Paul Flavell discovered a purely algebraic, local group-theoretic proof of the classical Baer-Suzuki theorem that completely avoided character theory and did not invoke the Classification of Finite Simple Groups (CFSG).

In the modern era (2010s–2020s), research shifted to quantitative and probabilistic variations. S. Guest, R. Guralnick, and G. Malle proved "Quantitative Baer-Suzuki" theorems, showing that one often only needs to check a small, bounded number of random conjugates to determine if an element lies in the $p$-core. Recently, probabilistic Baer-Suzuki limits have been solved, providing sharp lower bounds on the probability that $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is a $p$-group for $x \notin O_p(G)$.

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

- **Finite Groups:** The classical statement is universally proven for all finite groups and all primes $p$.
- **Quantitative Bounds for Simple Groups:** It has been verified that for a finite simple group $G$ and a $p$-element $x \in G$, there almost always exists a single appropriately chosen $g \in G$ such that $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is not a $p$-group, except for highly specific exceptions in Lie type groups defined over small fields.
- **Locally Finite Groups:** The classical theorem holds for locally finite groups. If $G$ is a locally finite group and $x$ is a $p$-element such that $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is a finite $p$-group for all $g \in G$, then the normal closure of $x$ is a locally finite $p$-group.
- **Algebraic Groups:** Solved for linear algebraic groups. If $x$ is a unipotent element and $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is unipotent for all $g$ in a Zariski dense subset, then $x$ is in the unipotent radical $R_u(G)$.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

Extending the Baer-Suzuki theorem to arbitrary infinite groups or deriving completely uniform deterministic bounds for randomized algorithms hits fundamental limits:
- **Lack of Local-to-Global Induction:** For arbitrary (not locally finite) infinite groups, finitely generated subgroups (like $\langle x, x^g \rangle$) being $p$-groups (the Burnside property) does not guarantee that their normal closure is well-behaved, due to the existence of infinite Tarski monster groups.
- **Exceptional Geometries:** In quantitative versions for finite groups of Lie type over characteristic $p$, the parabolic subgroup structures create dense "traps" of unipotent elements. Standard random walks on the group manifold mix too slowly to guarantee hitting a $g$ where $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is not a $p$-group within $O(1)$ steps.

## 6. The Gap

The boundary between verified truth and remaining open questions lies almost entirely in *profinite groups* and *algorithmic group theory*. 
While the quantitative theorems prove the existence of $g_1, \dots, g_k$ such that $\langle x, x^{g_1}, \dots, x^{g_k} \rangle$ breaks the $p$-group property for $x \notin O_p(G)$, finding a constructive, deterministic algebraic geometry algorithm to select these $g_i$ in $O(\log |G|)$ time for arbitrary black-box simple groups is the last unresolved barrier.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Current active research focuses on the intersection of the Baer-Suzuki theorem and randomized group computation:
- **Black-Box Group Algorithms:** Researchers at RWTH Aachen and Imperial College London are using quantitative Baer-Suzuki results to design Las Vegas algorithms that compute the $p$-core of a black-box group in nearly linear time.
- **Profinite Group Analogues:** Investigating if probabilistic Baer-Suzuki theorems can map directly onto the Haar measure of profinite groups, specifically branch groups and pro-$p$ groups *(frontier — verify)*. 
- **The Solvable Radical:** Generalizing the two-element generation condition beyond $p$-groups to identify the solvable radical $R(G)$, using bounded commutators.

## 8. Future Work

Leading mathematicians suggest extending the probabilistic Baer-Suzuki framework to infinite topological groups with well-behaved measures. Specifically, determining the threshold probability $c > 0$ such that if $\mathbb{P}(\langle x, x^g \rangle \text{ is a } p\text{-group}) > c$, then $x \in O_p(G)$ is true unconditionally for any compact topological group. Furthermore, identifying tight lower bounds for these constants across all sporadic groups remains an open computational challenge.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

Consider the symmetric group on three letters, $G = S_3$, and the prime $p = 2$.
The order of $G$ is 6. The 2-core $O_2(S_3)$ is the largest normal 2-subgroup. Since the only normal subgroups of $S_3$ are the trivial group $\{e\}$ and $A_3$ (of order 3), we have $O_2(S_3) = \{e\}$.

Let us test a 2-element to see if the Baer-Suzuki condition correctly identifies that it is *not* in the 2-core.
Let $x = (1\;2)$, which is an element of order $2 = p^1$. 
According to the Baer-Suzuki theorem, since $x \notin O_2(S_3)$, there must exist some $g \in G$ such that $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ is **not** a 2-group.

Let us test conjugates:
- If $g = e$, $x^g = (1\;2)$. The group $\langle (1\;2), (1\;2) \rangle = \langle (1\;2) \rangle$ has order 2, which is a 2-group.
- If $g = (1\;3)$, we compute the conjugate $x^g$:
  $$x^g = (1\;3)^{-1} (1\;2) (1\;3) = (1\;3)(1\;2)(1\;3) = (2\;3)$$
Now consider the group generated by $x$ and $x^g$:
  $$H = \langle (1\;2), (2\;3) \rangle$$
Since $(1\;2)$ and $(2\;3)$ are transpositions that generate the entirety of $S_3$, we have $H = S_3$.
The order of $H$ is $6$, which is not a power of $2$. Thus, $H$ is not a 2-group. 

This explicitly demonstrates the theorem: because $\langle x, x^g \rangle$ failed to be a 2-group for $g = (1\;3)$, we conclude $x = (1\;2) \notin O_2(S_3)$, precisely matching the fact that $O_2(S_3) = \{e\}$.
