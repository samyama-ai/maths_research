# Eisenbud-Green-Harris Conjecture

> **Topic:** Algebra & Group Theory · **ID:** `02-algebra-group-theory/eisenbud-green-harris-conjecture` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

The Eisenbud-Green-Harris (EGH) conjecture is a central open problem in commutative algebra that generalizes Macaulay's theorem on the growth of Hilbert functions. 

Let $K$ be a field, and let $S = K[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be the polynomial ring in $n$ variables over $K$, equipped with the standard grading. Suppose that $I \subset S$ is a homogeneous ideal that contains a regular sequence of homogeneous polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_r$ of degrees $d_1, \dots, d_r$, ordered such that $d_1 \le d_2 \le \dots \le d_r$. 

The EGH conjecture asserts that the Hilbert function of the quotient ring $S/I$ is bounded by the Hilbert function of a specific monomial ideal called a lex-plus-powers (LPP) ideal. Specifically, the conjecture states that there exists a lexicographic ideal $J \subset S$ such that if we define the LPP ideal as $L = J + (x_1^{d_1}, \dots, x_r^{d_r})$, then the Hilbert functions are identical:
$$H(S/I, d) = H(S/L, d) \quad \text{for all } d \ge 0.$$

In practical terms, a complete proof of the conjecture requires demonstrating that the combinatorial upper bounds on the dimension of the graded components of an ideal—when constrained by the presence of a generic regular sequence—are entirely determined by the worst-case scenario, which is achieved when the regular sequence consists purely of monomials (i.e., pure powers of the variables).

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

The conjecture relies on several fundamental structures within combinatorial commutative algebra. 

Let $S = K[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be a polynomial ring. The ring is decomposed into graded components $S = \bigoplus_{d \ge 0} S_d$, where $S_d$ is the $K$-vector space spanned by all monomials of total degree $d$. For a homogeneous ideal $I = \bigoplus_{d \ge 0} I_d$, the quotient ring $S/I$ inherits this grading.

**Hilbert Function:**
The Hilbert function $H(M, \cdot) : \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ of a finitely generated graded $S$-module $M = \bigoplus_{d \ge 0} M_d$ measures the dimension of its graded components over the base field $K$:
$$H(M, d) = \dim_K(M_d)$$

**Regular Sequence:**
A sequence of homogeneous polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_r \in S$ is a regular sequence if the ideal generated by them, $(f_1, \dots, f_r)$, is a proper ideal of $S$, and for every $1 \le i \le r$, the element $f_i$ is a non-zerodivisor on the quotient ring $S / (f_1, \dots, f_{i-1})$. Geometrically, an ideal generated by a regular sequence corresponds to a complete intersection scheme.

**Lexicographic Order:**
The lexicographic (lex) order $>_{\text{lex}}$ on the monomials of $S$ is defined such that $x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_n^{a_n} >_{\text{lex}} x_1^{b_1} \cdots x_n^{b_n}$ if the first non-zero entry from the left in the difference vector $(a_1 - b_1, \dots, a_n - b_n)$ is strictly positive.

**Lex Ideals and Lex-Plus-Powers (LPP) Ideals:**
A monomial ideal $J \subset S$ is a lex ideal if, for every degree $d \ge 0$, whenever a monomial $m$ belongs to $J_d$ and $m' \in S_d$ is another monomial satisfying $m' >_{\text{lex}} m$, then $m'$ must also belong to $J_d$. 
A lex-plus-powers (LPP) ideal is a monomial ideal of the form $L = J + (x_1^{d_1}, \dots, x_r^{d_r})$, where $J$ is a lex ideal.

**Macaulay's Theorem (1927):**
Macaulay’s foundational theorem states that for any homogeneous ideal $I \subset S$, there exists a unique lex ideal $I^{\text{lex}}$ such that $H(S/I, d) = H(S/I^{\text{lex}}, d)$ for all $d \ge 0$. Macaulay completely characterized the numerical functions that can occur as the Hilbert function of a standard graded algebra. The EGH conjecture attempts to refine Macaulay's bounds by incorporating the constraint that $I$ must contain a regular sequence.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

The Eisenbud-Green-Harris conjecture was formulated in 1993 by David Eisenbud, Mark Green, and Joe Harris in their seminal paper *"Higher Castelnuovo Theory"*, published in *Astérisque*. 

The conjecture was deeply motivated by classical algebraic geometry, specifically the Cayley-Bacharach theorem. The classical theorem states that if a cubic algebraic curve in the projective plane $\mathbb{P}^2$ passes through eight of the nine intersection points of two other cubic curves, it is mathematically forced to pass through the ninth point. Algebraically, this imposes severe constraints on the dimension of the vector space of polynomials of a given degree that vanish on a zero-dimensional complete intersection. Eisenbud, Green, and Harris sought to generalize this incidence geometry to complete intersections of arbitrary dimensions and degrees, realizing that the bounds on the Hilbert function were the algebraic linchpin.

Historically, two extreme cases of the problem were already solved before the conjecture was explicitly named. Macaulay's theorem (1927) provided the tightest possible bounds for an unconstrained homogeneous ideal. Decades later, G.F. Clements and B. Lindström (1969) proved a sweeping generalization of Macaulay's theorem for quotient rings of the form $S / (x_1^{d_1}, \dots, x_n^{d_n})$. The Clements-Lindström theorem effectively proves the EGH conjecture for the special case where the regular sequence is strictly monomial (i.e., pure powers).

The overarching intuition behind the EGH conjecture is that generic regular sequences should not allow for more "algebraic freedom" (and therefore cannot have a larger Hilbert function) than the most combinatorially constrained regular sequences—the monomial regular sequences studied by Clements and Lindström. Despite decades of intense study, the conjecture remains open in the general case.

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

While the general conjecture remains elusive, substantial progress has been made in verifying specific dimensional and parametric cases:

- **Two Variables ($n=2$):** The conjecture is known to hold completely for polynomial rings in two variables. This was understood early on and follows from the structural rigidity of ideals in two variables and the existence of universal deformations.
- **Fast-Growing Degrees:** In 2008, Giulio Caviglia and Diane Maclagan published a breakthrough result proving the EGH conjecture for regular sequences where the degrees grow sufficiently fast. Specifically, they proved the conjecture holds if the degrees $d_j$ satisfy the condition $d_j > \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} (d_i - 1)$ for all $j > 1$.
- **Products of Linear Forms:** Work by Abedelfatah (2012) and Cooper proved that the conjecture holds if the regular sequence $f_1, \dots, f_r$ completely splits into products of linear forms. 
- **Quadrics:** Extensive empirical and theoretical work has been dedicated to the case where the regular sequence consists entirely of quadrics (i.e., $d_1 = d_2 = \dots = d_n = 2$). The conjecture has been verified for ideals containing regular sequences of quadrics in rings of up to $n \le 5$ variables, but remains open for arbitrary dimensions.
- **Almost Complete Intersections:** The conjecture holds for almost complete intersections (ideals generated by $n+1$ elements in $n$ variables) under strict degree constraints.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

The enduring difficulty of the EGH conjecture stems from the failure of standard commutative algebra and algebraic geometry toolkits to translate algebraic structure into combinatorial bounds without destructive side effects.

The most standard technique for bounding Hilbert functions is Gröbner basis theory. Given an ideal $I$, one typically passes to its initial ideal $\operatorname{in}_<(I)$ with respect to a term order. By Macaulay's theorem, passing to the initial ideal preserves the Hilbert function precisely. However, the operation behaves pathologically with respect to regular sequences. If $f_1, \dots, f_r$ is a regular sequence in $I$, their initial terms $\operatorname{in}_<(f_1), \dots, \operatorname{in}_<(f_r)$ almost never form a regular sequence in $\operatorname{in}_<(I)$. Thus, the very hypothesis that provides the restrictive power of the EGH conjecture—the existence of the regular sequence—is destroyed when mapping the problem into the combinatorial domain where Clements-Lindström applies.

Furthermore, attempts to use homological algebra or algebraic topology fall short because homological invariants are highly sensitive to continuous deformations. The graded Betti numbers, which encode the free resolution of the ideal, jump semicontinuously under specialization. Consequently, it is remarkably difficult to establish universal homological bounds that map a generic complete intersection reliably to a lex-plus-powers structure without losing track of the precise dimensions of the graded components in intermediate degrees.

## 6. The Gap

The precise mathematical gap lies between the combinatorics of monomial complete intersections and the algebra of generic complete intersections. The Clements-Lindström theorem provides an airtight, purely combinatorial proof for ideals containing monomial regular sequences. The gap is the absence of a universal deformation functor or a stable homological invariant that can strictly bound the growth of an arbitrary regular sequence against a monomial one, while circumventing the fact that generic initial ideal operations inherently obliterate the regular sequence property. Bridging this gap requires either discovering a novel Gröbner-like degeneration that preserves regular sequences (which is theorized to be impossible in the general case) or developing an entirely new framework for bounding Hilbert functions that bypasses term orders altogether.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Active research on the EGH conjecture is highly concentrated among commutative algebraists at institutions such as Purdue University, the University of Genoa, and various Max Planck Institutes. 

Recent literature focuses heavily on two fronts. First, researchers are attempting to systematically relax the Caviglia-Maclagan "fast-growing" bounds to encompass tighter degree sequences. Second, there is intense focus on the stronger **Lex-plus-powers (LPP) conjecture**, which posits that LPP ideals maximize the graded Betti numbers among all ideals with a fixed Hilbert function and a regular sequence. Since Betti numbers strictly determine the Hilbert function, proving the LPP conjecture would immediately resolve the EGH conjecture. 

*(frontier — verify)* Recent preprints have begun exploring characteristic $p > 0$ methods. By examining the action of the Frobenius endomorphism on local cohomology modules, some groups are attempting to establish asymptotic bounds on Hilbert functions that could potentially be lifted back to characteristic zero to resolve the EGH conjecture for quadrics.

## 8. Future Work

Leading mathematicians have outlined several strategic pathways for future research:

1.  **Resolution of the Quadric Case:** Proving the conjecture for regular sequences of quadrics ($d_1 = \dots = d_n = 2$) in all dimensions $n \ge 6$. Because quadrics represent the most tightly constrained complete intersections, this is widely viewed as the most significant, achievable milestone.
2.  **The LPP Betti Number Bounds:** Shifting focus from purely bounding the Hilbert function (vector space dimensions) to bounding the syzygies (Betti numbers). If it can be shown that lex-plus-powers ideals represent the extremal boundary of syzygy growth, the EGH conjecture will follow as a direct algebraic corollary.
3.  **Extremal Combinatorics Translations:** Translating the algebraic constraints of complete intersections into purely combinatorial questions using generalizations of the Kruskal-Katona theorem, mimicking the successful approach used by Macaulay.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

To ground the EGH conjecture, consider a specific instance in the polynomial ring with two variables, $S = K[x_1, x_2]$, where the base field $K = \mathbb{C}$. We will evaluate a regular sequence of quadrics, meaning $d_1 = 2$ and $d_2 = 2$.

Let our homogeneous ideal be $I = (x_1^2 + x_2^2, x_1 x_2)$. 
First, we verify that $f_1 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ and $f_2 = x_1 x_2$ form a regular sequence. The polynomial $f_1$ is non-zero, so it is a non-zerodivisor in $S$. In the quotient ring $S/(f_1)$, we must check if $f_2$ is a non-zerodivisor. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $f_1$ factors as $(x_1 + i x_2)(x_1 - i x_2)$. If $f_2 \cdot g = 0$ in $S/(f_1)$, then $x_1 x_2 g = h(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ for some polynomial $h \in S$. Because $x_1^2 + x_2^2$ shares no common factors with $x_1$ or $x_2$, unique factorization dictates that $x_1^2 + x_2^2$ must divide $g$. Thus, $g = 0$ in $S/(f_1)$, proving $f_2$ is a non-zerodivisor and the sequence is regular.

The EGH conjecture claims that the Hilbert function of $S/I$ is bounded by (and in this exact case, equal to) the Hilbert function of an LPP ideal $L = J + (x_1^2, x_2^2)$. Because the pure powers $(x_1^2, x_2^2)$ already generate an ideal with the minimal possible dimension for a sequence of two quadrics, we can set the lex ideal $J = (0)$, yielding the LPP ideal $L = (x_1^2, x_2^2)$.

Let us compute and compare the Hilbert functions $H(S/I, d)$ and $H(S/L, d)$ degree by degree:

- **Degree 0:** $S_0$ is spanned by $1$. Neither $I$ nor $L$ has elements of degree 0. Both quotients have dimension $1$.
- **Degree 1:** $S_1$ is spanned by $x_1, x_2$. Neither $I$ nor $L$ has elements of degree 1. Both quotients have dimension $2$.
- **Degree 2:** $S_2$ is spanned by $x_1^2, x_1 x_2, x_2^2$. 
  - For $S/I$, we quotient by the relations $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 0$ (meaning $x_1^2 \equiv -x_2^2$) and $x_1 x_2 = 0$. This leaves a single basis element, $x_2^2$, yielding dimension $1$.
  - For $S/L$, we quotient by $x_1^2 = 0$ and $x_2^2 = 0$. This leaves a single basis element, $x_1 x_2$, yielding dimension $1$.
- **Degree 3:** $S_3$ is generated by $x_1^3, x_1^2 x_2, x_1 x_2^2, x_2^3$. 
  - For $I$, the degree 3 component $I_3$ is generated by multiplying the degree 2 generators by $x_1$ and $x_2$. We get $x_1^3 + x_1 x_2^2$, $x_1^2 x_2 + x_2^3$, $x_1^2 x_2$, and $x_1 x_2^2$. These span all of $S_3$, so the quotient dimension is $0$.
  - For $L$, the degree 3 component $L_3$ is generated by $x_1^3, x_1^2 x_2, x_1 x_2^2, x_2^3$. This also spans all of $S_3$, so the quotient dimension is $0$.

Thus, the Hilbert function for both $S/I$ and $S/L$ is the sequence $(1, 2, 1, 0, 0, \dots)$, perfectly matching the EGH conjecture's assertion that the generic regular sequence $I$ is bounded precisely by the combinatorics of the pure powers ideal $L$.
