# Eremenko Conjecture

> **Topic:** 05-analysis · **ID:** `05-analysis/eremenko-conjecture` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

The Eremenko Conjecture is a fundamental question in one-dimensional complex dynamics concerning the topological structure of the set of points that escape to infinity under the iteration of a transcendental entire function. 

Formulated by Alexandre Eremenko in 1989, the original general conjecture states:
**For every transcendental entire function $f: \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$, every connected component of the escaping set $I(f)$ is unbounded.**

A widely studied, more restrictive variant known as the **Strong Eremenko Conjecture** posits that every point in $I(f)$ can be connected to infinity by a continuous path (often called a "dynamic ray" or "hair") lying entirely within the escaping set.

In 2021, the original general conjecture was definitively proven false by David Martí-Pete, Lasse Rempe, and James Waterman, who constructed a transcendental entire function possessing bounded escaping components. However, the conjecture remains one of the most significant strictly open problems when restricted to the **Eremenko-Lyubich class $\mathcal{B}$**—the space of entire functions with a bounded set of singular values. Because functions in class $\mathcal{B}$ govern the most analytically well-behaved subset of transcendental dynamics, modern research rephrases the open problem as follows:

**Modern Formulation:** Does the Eremenko Conjecture hold for all functions $f \in \mathcal{B}$? Equivalently, does the strict global restriction of a bounded singular set preclude the existence of bounded connected components in the escaping set?

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

Let $f: \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ be a transcendental entire function, meaning it is holomorphic everywhere on the complex plane and has an essential singularity at infinity. The study of the discrete dynamical system generated by the iterates $f^n = f \circ f \circ \dots \circ f$ partitions the complex plane into two fundamental sets:
1. The **Fatou set** $F(f)$, defined as the maximal open set where the family of iterates $\{f^n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ forms a normal family (exhibiting stable, predictable behavior).
2. The **Julia set** $J(f) = \mathbb{C} \setminus F(f)$, which is the closure of the repelling periodic points (exhibiting chaotic dynamics).

The central object of the conjecture is the **escaping set**, defined formally as:
$$I(f) := \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : f^n(z) \to \infty \text{ as } n \to \infty \}$$

Eremenko proved several foundational properties of $I(f)$:
* $I(f) \neq \emptyset$.
* $I(f) \cap J(f) \neq \emptyset$, and in fact, the boundary of the escaping set is exactly the Julia set: $\partial I(f) = J(f)$.
* The closure of the escaping set, $\overline{I(f)}$, possesses no bounded connected components.

The geometry of $I(f)$ is deeply constrained by the distribution of the function's **singular values**. A point $w \in \mathbb{C}$ is a singular value of $f$ if it is either a critical value (there exists $z \in \mathbb{C}$ such that $f(z) = w$ and $f'(z) = 0$) or an asymptotic value (there exists a curve $\gamma(t) \to \infty$ such that $\lim_{t \to \infty} f(\gamma(t)) = w$). We denote the closure of the set of all singular values by $S(f)$. 

The **Eremenko-Lyubich class** is defined as:
$$\mathcal{B} := \{ f : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C} \mid f \text{ is transcendental entire and } S(f) \text{ is bounded} \}$$

A subclass of $\mathcal{B}$ is the Speiser class $\mathcal{S}$, which contains functions where $S(f)$ is finite. 

The analytical intractability of the escaping set often scales with the function's **order of growth**, defined as:
$$\rho(f) = \limsup_{r \to \infty} \frac{\log \log M(r,f)}{\log r}$$
where $M(r,f) = \max_{|z|=r} |f(z)|$ is the maximum modulus function. The open gap in the conjecture lies specifically in the regime where $f \in \mathcal{B}$ and $\rho(f) = \infty$.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

The topological structure of $I(f)$ was largely ignored until Eremenko's 1989 paper (*On the iteration of entire functions*). Before this, complex dynamics focused predominantly on polynomials and rational functions, where the escaping set is simply the basin of attraction for the super-attracting fixed point at infinity (and thus trivially an open, connected, unbounded domain). For transcendental functions, infinity is an essential singularity, making $I(f)$ highly fractured and totally disconnected in the traditional sense, yet paradoxically containing unbounded structures.

Throughout the 1990s and 2000s, the "Cantor bouquet" structure—where $I(f)$ consists of an uncountable union of disjoint curves extending to infinity—was discovered in the exponential family $E_\lambda(z) = \lambda e^z$. This heavily fueled the belief that the Strong Eremenko Conjecture was universally true, at least for class $\mathcal{B}$.

A major paradigm shift occurred in 2011 when Rottenfußer, Rückert, Rempe, and Schleicher published a watershed paper disproving the *Strong* Eremenko Conjecture. They constructed a function $f \in \mathcal{B}$ of infinite order for which $I(f)$ contains absolutely no non-trivial curves. The escaping components were totally disconnected, although they were unbounded, leaving the standard conjecture alive.

The ultimate blow to the general conjecture came in a 2021 preprint (published in *JAMS* in 2025). Martí-Pete, Rempe, and Waterman provided a strict counterexample to the standard Eremenko Conjecture. They engineered a transcendental entire function possessing an escaping set with bounded connected components, including bounded singletons. 

The state of the art has now collapsed entirely onto the Eremenko-Lyubich class $\mathcal{B}$. Because the 2025 counterexample fundamentally relies on escaping Fatou components (specifically, a pathological sequence of wandering domains), and because functions in $\mathcal{B}$ are proven to have no escaping Fatou components, the counterexample cannot be ported into class $\mathcal{B}$. Therefore, Eremenko's conjecture for class $\mathcal{B}$ remains one of the premier targets in complex analysis today.

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

While the general conjecture is false, it has been rigorously verified for several massive subclasses of transcendental entire functions:

*   **Finite Order Functions in $\mathcal{B}$**: In the same 2011 paper that disproved the strong conjecture for infinite order, Rottenfußer et al. proved that if $f \in \mathcal{B}$ and the order of growth $\rho(f) < \infty$, the Strong Eremenko conjecture holds. Every escaping point lies on a dynamic ray (an unbounded continuous curve in $I(f)$).
*   **The Speiser Class $\mathcal{S}$**: Because $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{B}$, finite-order functions in the Speiser class strictly satisfy the conjecture.
*   **Infinite Spiders' Webs**: Rippon and Stallard (2012) identified that for a large class of entire functions (particularly those with small growth, where $M(r,f)$ grows slower than $\exp((\log r)^A)$ for certain $A < 1$), the escaping set forms a topological "infinite spider's web". A spider's web is a highly connected topological space containing a sequence of nested, bounded, simply connected domains. When $I(f)$ is a spider's web, it is exactly one single unbounded connected component, trivially verifying Eremenko's conjecture.
*   **Compositional Classes**: The conjecture holds for any finite composition of polynomials and the exponential function. 
*   **Concrete Families**: The trigonometric families (e.g., $f(z) = \lambda \sin(z)$), the cosine family, and the exponential family have been exhaustively classified. Their escaping sets form Cantor bouquets, meaning every connected component is an unbounded ray.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

The difficulty in resolving the conjecture for the infinite-order regime of class $\mathcal{B}$ stems from the breakdown of classical analytic extension techniques and the extreme fractal pathology of infinite-degree covering maps.

**1. The Breakdown of the Logarithmic Transform:**
For functions in $\mathcal{B}$, one standard technique is to apply a logarithmic change of coordinates to construct dynamic rays. Let $D$ be a disk containing the bounded singular set $S(f)$. Then $f: \mathbb{C} \setminus f^{-1}(D) \to \mathbb{C} \setminus D$ is an unbranched covering map. Lifting this to logarithmic coordinates yields a $2\pi i$-periodic map $F: \mathbb{H} \to \mathbb{H}$ on a right half-plane. For finite-order functions, the derivative of $F$ is sufficiently controlled, allowing the pullbacks of horizontal lines to converge to continuous curves (rays). For infinite-order functions, the derivative of $F$ can fluctuate wildly. The curves "fold" and oscillate with such infinite frequency that their arc length diverges, and taking the limit of the pullbacks results in a totally disconnected set of points rather than a continuous curve.

**2. Arakelyan's Theorem and the Singular Set Barrier:**
To construct counterexamples (like that of Martí-Pete et al.), mathematicians use Arakelyan's Approximation Theorem, which allows one to define the dynamics of a function arbitrarily on a sequence of disjoint, closed "islands" (Arakelyan sets) and then approximate this behavior globally with a single entire function. The authors used this to construct wandering domains with "Lakes of Wada" boundaries. However, Arakelyan's theorem offers zero control over the global critical points or asymptotic values of the approximating function. Consequently, it is currently impossible to use Arakelyan approximation while simultaneously forcing $S(f)$ to remain bounded.

**3. Maverick Points:**
The bounded escaping components in the general counterexample exist due to "maverick points"—points on the boundary of an escaping wandering domain that accumulate at entirely different rates, or towards entirely different regions, than the domain itself. Bounding these maverick points isolates escaping trajectories. Since functions in $\mathcal{B}$ cannot possess escaping wandering domains, constructing maverick points in $\mathcal{B}$ requires operating strictly on the Julia set, which lacks the open topological volume necessary to easily hide isolated escaping orbits.

## 6. The Gap

The exact boundary between what is verified and what remains unproven is perfectly demarcated: **Transcendental entire functions $f \in \mathcal{B}$ with order of growth $\rho(f) = \infty$.**

We know that for these functions, the escaping components are not necessarily curves (the strong conjecture fails). We know that outside of $\mathcal{B}$, escaping components can be bounded (the general conjecture fails). The specific mathematical barrier that must be crossed is determining whether a bounded singular set $S(f)$ exerts enough global rigidity on the Julia set to prevent the formation of isolated, bounded escaping continua, or whether infinite order $\rho(f) = \infty$ provides enough local analytical freedom to simulate "Lakes of Wada" topologies purely within the Julia set. 

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Research in this domain is aggressively pursuing two distinct paradigms:

1. **Topological rigidities of Class $\mathcal{B}$**: Many researchers are attempting to prove the conjecture for $\mathcal{B}$ by investigating the potential theory and harmonic measure of components of $J(f)$. If one can prove that the absence of escaping Fatou components in $\mathcal{B}$ categorically forbids the topological structures that generate "maverick points", the conjecture would be validated. 
2. **Advanced Approximation Spaces *(frontier — verify)***: A competing school of thought is actively attempting to adapt Arakelyan-style approximations to restricted function spaces. Recent preprints have explored "quasiconformal folding" and modified Cauchy transforms to build entire functions that approximate targeted dynamics while actively suppressing the creation of extraneous singular values. If a constructive approximation theorem can be derived that enforces $f \in \mathcal{B}$, a counterexample is likely imminent.

## 8. Future Work

Leading mathematicians in transcendental dynamics (such as Rempe, Rippon, Stallard, and Sixsmith) have articulated several immediate research pathways:
*   **Resolution for the Speiser Class:** Before tackling all of $\mathcal{B}$, researchers aim to resolve the infinite-order gap for the Speiser class $\mathcal{S}$ (functions with finitely many singular values). This highly constrained space may offer rigidities that force unboundedness.
*   **Classification of Escaping Continua:** If bounded escaping components do exist in $\mathcal{B}$, what is their topological dimension? Can they be full continua, or must they be totally disconnected singletons?
*   **Meromorphic Generalization:** Expanding the study of $I(f)$ to transcendental meromorphic functions, where points can escape by accumulating at the poles rather than exclusively at infinity.
*   **The Maverick Point Framework:** Developing a standalone topological classification for "maverick points" on the boundaries of wandering domains, independent of the Eremenko conjecture.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

To ground the conjecture, we examine the canonical example that originally inspired it: the exponential function $E(z) = e^z$. 

We wish to show that $I(E)$ contains unbounded connected components. First, observe the behavior of $E(z)$ on the positive real axis. For any $x \in \mathbb{R}$ where $x > 0$, the sequence $x \mapsto e^x \mapsto e^{e^x} \dots$ grows strictly monotonically to infinity. Therefore, the entire ray $[0, \infty)$ is contained in the escaping set $I(E)$.

The geometry becomes more intricate off the real axis. Let $z = x + iy$. We have $E(z) = e^x(\cos y + i \sin y)$. The exponential map takes horizontal strips of width $2\pi$ and wraps them around the complex plane. Consider the horizontal strip $S_0 = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : -\pi/2 < \text{Im}(z) < \pi/2 \}$. For $z \in S_0$, the real part of $E(z)$ is strictly positive, meaning iteration within this strip continuously drives the modulus to infinity. 

By taking careful branches of the logarithm, one can track the pre-images of the escaping real axis. Devaney and Krych (1984) formally demonstrated that for every escaping point $z_0 \in I(E)$, there exists a continuous curve $\gamma: [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{C}$ such that:
1. $\gamma(0) = z_0$
2. $\gamma(t) \in I(E)$ for all $t \geq 0$
3. $\lim_{t \to \infty} \gamma(t) = \infty$

This topological structure is called a **Cantor bouquet**—an uncountable collection of disjoint, unbounded curves ("hairs") stretching to infinity. Because every point in $I(E)$ lies on one of these unbounded continuous hairs, $E(z)$ perfectly satisfies both the general Eremenko Conjecture and the Strong Eremenko Conjecture. 

This is structurally consistent with modern theorems because $E(z)$ has only one singular value (the asymptotic value $0$), meaning $S(E) = \{0\}$ is bounded. Thus, $E(z) \in \mathcal{B}$. Furthermore, its order of growth is $\rho(E) = 1$, placing it squarely in the finite-order regime of class $\mathcal{B}$ where the Strong Conjecture is rigorously verified to hold.
