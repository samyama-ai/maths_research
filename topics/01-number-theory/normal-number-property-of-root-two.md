# Normal Number Property of Root Two

> **Topic:** 01-number-theory · **ID:** `01-number-theory/normal-number-property-of-root-two` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

Is $\sqrt{2}$ a normal number in base 10? More generally, the conjecture states that every irrational algebraic number is absolutely normal (normal in every integer base $b \ge 2$). 

The conjecture asserts that the sequence of digits of $\sqrt{2}$ in any integer base behaves identically to a sequence of independent uniform random variables. A complete proof requires demonstrating that for any integer base $b \ge 2$ and any integer $k \ge 1$, every possible word of length $k$ utilizing the alphabet $\{0, 1, \dots, b-1\}$ appears in the base-$b$ expansion of $\sqrt{2}$ with an exact asymptotic frequency of $b^{-k}$. 

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b \ge 2$ be an integer. The fractional part of $x$ can be expanded in base $b$ as:
$$ x = \lfloor x \rfloor + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n b^{-n} $$
where the digits $a_n \in \{0, 1, \dots, b-1\}$.

For a finite word $w = d_1 d_2 \dots d_k$ of length $|w| = k$ over the alphabet $\Sigma_b = \{0, \dots, b-1\}$, let $N_w(x, N)$ denote the number of times the word $w$ appears as a contiguous subword within the first $N$ digits of the fractional part of $x$. The real number $x$ is defined as **normal in base $b$** if, for all lengths $k \ge 1$ and all words $w \in \Sigma_b^k$, the asymptotic frequency satisfies:
$$ \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{N_w(x, N)}{N} = \frac{1}{b^k} $$

A number is **absolutely normal** if it is normal in every integer base $b \ge 2$.

Equivalently, normality can be framed via dynamical systems and uniform distribution modulo 1. By Weyl's Criterion, $x$ is normal in base $b$ if and only if the sequence $(b^n x)_{n \ge 1}$ is uniformly distributed modulo 1. This means that for every Riemann integrable function $f: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$,
$$ \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N f(\{b^n x\}) = \int_0^1 f(t) \, dt $$
where $\{y\} = y - \lfloor y \rfloor$ denotes the fractional part of $y$.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

Émile Borel formalized the concept of normal numbers in 1909. Using the Borel-Cantelli lemma, he proved that Lebesgue-almost every real number is absolutely normal. Despite this measure-theoretic abundance, Borel famously noted the extreme mathematical difficulty of proving normality for any specific, naturally occurring irrational number, such as $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$, or $e$.

Historically, the only proven normal numbers are those explicitly constructed for that purpose (such as Champernowne's constant $0.1234567891011\dots$, proven normal in base 10 in 1933). Conversely, establishing the normality of algebraic irrationals like $\sqrt{2}$ remains profoundly resistant to theoretical progress. 

The State of the Art is highly bifurcated: while computational investigations have heavily supported the conjecture (the decimal and binary digits of $\sqrt{2}$ have been verified to trillions of places and pass all statistical tests for uniform randomness), purely theoretical verification remains essentially at zero. 

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

There are no verified cases of any irrational algebraic number being proven normal in any base. We do not even know if $\sqrt{2}$ is "simply normal" in base 10 (where each single digit 0-9 would appear with a frequency of exactly 1/10). Mathematically, it has not even been ruled out that the decimal expansion of $\sqrt{2}$ eventually stops producing the digit '7'.

However, partial results have established strict lower bounds on the complexity of algebraic numbers:
- **Block Complexity Bounds:** Let $p(n)$ be the block complexity function (the number of distinct words of length $n$ occurring in the base-$b$ expansion of an irrational algebraic number $x$). Adamczewski and Bugeaud (2007), relying on the $p$-adic Schmidt Subspace Theorem, proved that $\lim \inf_{n \to \infty} p(n)/n = \infty$. This ruled out expansions being eventually periodic or generated by finite automata, though it remains astronomically short of the $b^n$ bound required for normal numbers.
- **Digit Density Bounds:** Bailey, Borwein, Crandall, and Pomerance (2004) proved that the number of non-zero digits in the first $N$ bits of the binary expansion of a real algebraic number of degree $d$ grows at least as $C \cdot N^{1/d}$. While a breakthrough, this lower bound falls vastly short of the $N/2$ density required to prove normality in base 2.
- **Empirical Cases:** Computationally, $\sqrt{2}$ is verified to be completely normal in base 10 and base 2 up to at least $N = 10^{13}$ decimal places via rigorous statistical testing on large computing clusters.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

The fundamental bottleneck is the severe mathematical disconnect between algebraic properties (being the root of an integer polynomial like $x^2 - 2 = 0$) and base-$b$ digit expansions (which are governed by the ergodic properties of the map $T(x) = bx \bmod 1$). 

Current tools from Diophantine approximation (such as Roth's Theorem or Baker's method) are highly effective at bounding how closely rational fractions approximate $\sqrt{2}$, which indirectly bounds the maximal length of contiguous blocks of zeros. However, these techniques are fundamentally blind to the combinatorial structure of mixed-digit blocks. 

Attempting to resolve the problem via Harmonic Analysis (using Weyl's Criterion) requires establishing non-trivial upper bounds on exponential sums of the form:
$$ \sum_{n=1}^N e^{2\pi i h b^n \sqrt{2}} $$
Because $b^n \sqrt{2}$ grows exponentially, the sequence is deeply lacunary. Standard analytic techniques, like van der Corput's method of differencing, collapse entirely when applied to rapidly growing exponential sequences without a structurally predictable algebraic geometry to control the fractional parts $\{b^n \sqrt{2}\}$.

## 6. The Gap

The exact mathematical gap lies between establishing *super-linear combinatorial complexity* and achieving *maximal measure-theoretic complexity*. 

Currently, mathematics can prove that the expansion of $\sqrt{2}$ is sufficiently complex to avoid being modeled by a finite-state machine. To bridge the gap, one must prove that the empirical measure corresponding to its digit orbit:
$$ \mu_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \delta_{\{b^n \sqrt{2}\}} $$
converges weakly to the Lebesgue measure on $[0,1]$ as $N \to \infty$. Crossing this barrier demands a fundamentally new theoretical paradigm that fuses arithmetic geometry, transcendental number theory, and smooth ergodic theory, allowing integer polynomials to interact constructively with modular shifting operators.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Active research on the normal number property follows several specialized trajectories:
- **Topological Dynamics and the B-C Hypothesis:** Researchers continue probing the Bailey-Crandall hypothesis, which proposes an equivalency between the normality of algebraic numbers and the pseudo-random properties of specific chaotic dynamical systems. 
- **Automata and Transcendence:** Building on the foundations of the Schmidt Subspace Theorem, groups are attempting to push the lower bounds on the complexity function $p(n)$ from super-linear ($O(n \log n)$) toward exponential bounds. 
- **Measure Rigidity:** Explorations into Furstenberg’s $\times 2, \times 3$ theorem are attempting to leverage the joint distribution of digits in multiplicatively independent bases. * *(frontier — verify)* * Recent preprints attempt to deploy effective ergodic theorems on homogenous spaces to bound the discrepancy of the sequence of fractional parts of low-degree algebraic numbers, mapping the digits to orbits on moduli spaces.

## 8. Future Work

Leading figures in Diophantine approximation, such as Yann Bugeaud and Michel Waldschmidt, emphasize tackling intermediate properties before attempting full normality. A primary target is proving that the binary expansion of $\sqrt{2}$ contains a strictly positive asymptotic density of 1-bits (i.e., proving the density is $>0\%$ rather than exactly $50\%$). 

Another key strategy is the investigation of morphic words and block substitutions: by proving that no irrational algebraic number can be the fixed point of any non-trivial block substitution rule, one could systematically rule out massive classes of non-normal behaviors.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

To ground the conjecture in reality, consider the base-10 expansion of $\sqrt{2}$:
$$ \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142135623730950488\dots $$

We examine its fractional part $x = \sqrt{2} - 1 = 0.4142135623\dots$ which corresponds to the dynamical system $T(y) = 10y \bmod 1$. Finding the digits is equivalent to applying $T$ and recording the integer part extracted.
- **Step 1:** $10 \times 0.414213\dots = 4.14213\dots \implies a_1 = 4$, $T(x) = 0.14213\dots$
- **Step 2:** $10 \times 0.14213\dots = 1.42135\dots \implies a_2 = 1$, $T^2(x) = 0.42135\dots$
- **Step 3:** $10 \times 0.42135\dots = 4.21356\dots \implies a_3 = 4$, $T^3(x) = 0.21356\dots$

If we examine the first $N = 10$ digits of the fractional part: `4, 1, 4, 2, 1, 3, 5, 6, 2, 3`. 
The empirical frequencies for this microscopic sample are:
- '1' appears 2 times (20%)
- '2' appears 2 times (20%)
- '3' appears 2 times (20%)
- '4' appears 2 times (20%)
- '5' appears 1 time (10%)
- '6' appears 1 time (10%)
- '0', '7', '8', '9' appear 0 times (0%)

The conjecture dictates that as $N \to \infty$ (e.g., examining $N = 10^{13}$ digits), the frequency of every single digit $0$ through $9$ will converge exactly to $1/10$. Furthermore, every 2-digit block (e.g., '14' or '07') will appear exactly $1/100$ of the time. However, due to the failure of existing mathematical machinery, we cannot theoretically prove that the digit '7' ever appears a single time beyond the known computed ranges.
