# Ankeny-Artin-Chowla Conjecture

> **Topic:** Number Theory · **ID:** `01-number-theory/ankeny-artin-chowla-conjecture` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

Let $p$ be a prime number such that $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Let $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p})$ be the corresponding real quadratic number field. The fundamental unit of the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ can be written in the form:
$$ \epsilon = \frac{t + u\sqrt{p}}{2} > 1 $$
where $t$ and $u$ are positive integers. 

The **Ankeny-Artin-Chowla Conjecture** states that the prime $p$ does not divide the integer coefficient $u$. Equivalently, the conjecture asserts that:
$$ u \not\equiv 0 \pmod p $$
A complete proof would require unconditionally establishing this non-divisibility for all primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, while a disproof requires finding a single prime $p$ for which $p \mid u$.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

The conjecture is grounded in algebraic number theory, specifically the study of real quadratic fields and their unit groups.

*   **Real Quadratic Field and Ring of Integers:** For a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the real quadratic field is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p})$. Its ring of integers is $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1 + \sqrt{p}}{2}\right]$.
*   **Dirichlet's Unit Theorem:** The group of units in $\mathcal{O}_K$, denoted $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$, is isomorphic to $\{\pm 1\} \times \mathbb{Z}$. It is generated by a single fundamental unit $\epsilon > 1$.
*   **Norm Equation (Pell's Equation):** The field norm of the fundamental unit $N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\epsilon)$ must be $\pm 1$. For prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, it is known that $N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\epsilon) = -1$. Writing $\epsilon = \frac{t + u\sqrt{p}}{2}$, the norm yields the negative Pell-like equation:
    $$ \frac{t^2 - p u^2}{4} = -1 \implies t^2 - p u^2 = -4 $$
*   **The AAC Congruence:** The conjecture was motivated by a congruence proved by Ankeny, Artin, and Chowla in 1952 linking the fundamental unit to the class number $h(p)$ of $K$ and the Bernoulli numbers $B_k$. Specifically, they proved that:
    $$ h(p) \frac{u}{t} \equiv B_{(p-1)/2} \pmod p $$
    (Note: Indexing and exact rational factors vary by text, but this establishes the core relation). Assuming $p \nmid h(p)$ (which is believed to be virtually always true, parallel to Vandiver's conjecture), the conjecture $p \nmid u$ is algebraically equivalent to stating that $p$ does not divide the numerator of the Bernoulli number $B_{(p-1)/2}$.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

The conjecture was proposed in 1952 by Nesmith Ankeny, Emil Artin, and Sarvadaman Chowla in their landmark paper *The class-number of real quadratic number fields* published in the *Annals of Mathematics*. While investigating the relationships between class numbers and units, they observed that $p \nmid u$ held true for all accessible primes and formalized it as a conjecture.

In 1960, L. J. Mordell generalized the problem and investigated analogous questions for primes $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. For such primes, the fundamental unit takes the form $\epsilon = t + u\sqrt{p}$, and Mordell conjectured that $p \nmid u$ here as well. This extension is sometimes called the Mordell Conjecture (not to be confused with Faltings' theorem on algebraic curves).

The State of the Art remains largely computational. No theoretical framework has been found that systematically prevents $p \mid u$. Instead, advancements in distributed computing and algorithmic number theory have continuously pushed the verified lower bounds higher, relying on optimized algorithms for computing fundamental units and class groups.

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

To date, the conjecture has not been proven for any infinite family of primes. However, it has been rigorously verified computationally for vast ranges:
*   **Early Computations:** In 1982, F. J. van der Linden rigorously verified the conjecture for all primes $p < 10^5$. 
*   **Modern Verifications:** With the advent of better algorithms (such as the subexponential time algorithms for class groups) and raw computing power, the conjecture has been verified for all primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ up to $p < 10^{11}$, and unpublished distributed computing efforts have reportedly tested bounds up to $p < 10^{15}$. 
*   In all tested cases, no counterexample (where $p \mid u$) has been found. The generalized conjecture (Mordell's extension for $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$) also remains verified up to similar computational limits.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

The fundamental reason the problem remains unsolved is that there is no known algebraic, geometric, or topological obstruction preventing $p$ from dividing $u$. 

Number theorists frequently analyze this using the **Cohen-Lenstra heuristics**, which model class groups and related arithmetic invariants probabilistically. Under the assumption that the $p$-adic valuation of $u$ behaves like a random integer modulo $p$, the probability that $p \mid u$ for any given prime is roughly $\frac{1}{p}$. 

If this probabilistic heuristic is accurate, the expected number of counterexamples $C(X)$ up to a bound $X$ is governed by the sum of reciprocals of primes:
$$ \mathbb{E}[C(X)] \approx \sum_{p \le X, \, p \equiv 1 \,(4)} \frac{1}{p} \sim \frac{1}{2} \ln(\ln X) $$
Because the double logarithm grows extraordinarily slowly, finding zero counterexamples up to $10^{11}$ (where $\frac{1}{2} \ln(\ln 10^{11}) \approx 1.6$) is entirely consistent with the possibility that counterexamples *do* exist but are astronomically rare. Current mathematical techniques—such as Iwasawa theory, $p$-adic L-functions, and Galois cohomology—cannot easily distinguish between a rare statistical anomaly and a strict structural impossibility.

## 6. The Gap

The gap lies between computational evidence for small primes and the lack of a deterministic algebraic constraint. To fully resolve the conjecture, mathematics requires a breakthrough establishing a strict non-trivial lower bound on the $p$-adic valuation of the fundamental unit $\epsilon$. Specifically, researchers need a mechanism (likely stemming from deep properties of $p$-adic modular forms or Stark's conjectures) that explains why the arithmetic of Pell's equation $t^2 - p u^2 = -4$ completely excludes the possibility of $u$ being a multiple of $p$, overriding the pseudorandom distribution modulo $p$.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Current research approaches the Ankeny-Artin-Chowla conjecture through several lenses:
*   **$p$-adic Analytic Number Theory:** Exploring connections to the non-vanishing of $p$-adic $L$-functions and generalized Bernoulli numbers. 
*   **Cohen-Lenstra Refinements:** Theoretical work is being done to refine statistical heuristics, attempting to prove whether the uniform distribution assumption for $u \pmod p$ is genuinely valid or if hidden algebraic constraints skew the probabilities to zero.
*   **Massive GPU Computations:** * (frontier — verify)* New algorithmic implementations utilizing highly parallelized GPU clusters are attempting to push the verification bound toward $p \sim 10^{17}$, searching for the elusive first counterexample which would immediately settle the conjecture in the negative.

## 8. Future Work

Leading number theorists suggest the following pathways:
1.  **Unconditional bounds on class numbers:** Improving bounds on the $p$-part of the class group of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p})$ to completely untangle the $h(p)$ term from the AAC congruence.
2.  **Iwasawa Theory for Real Quadratic Fields:** Developing stronger Main Conjectures in Iwasawa theory for real quadratic fields that might restrict the algebraic structure of the units modulo $p$.
3.  **Search for Counterexamples:** Continued scaling of the computational limits. A counterexample, while disproving the conjecture, would provide invaluable data on the nature of exceptions in arithmetic statistics.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

To ground the abstract statement, consider the first two primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$: $p=5$ and $p=13$.

**Case 1: $p = 5$**
*   The field is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$.
*   The fundamental unit $\epsilon = \frac{t + u\sqrt{5}}{2}$ must satisfy $t^2 - 5u^2 = -4$.
*   By inspection, $t=1, u=1$ yields $1^2 - 5(1^2) = -4$.
*   Thus, $\epsilon = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$ (the Golden Ratio).
*   Here $u = 1$. We check the conjecture: Does $5$ divide $1$? No ($1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod 5$). The conjecture holds.

**Case 2: $p = 13$**
*   The field is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{13})$.
*   We solve the norm equation $t^2 - 13u^2 = -4$.
*   Testing small integers, if $u=1$, then $t^2 - 13 = -4 \implies t^2 = 9 \implies t = 3$.
*   Thus, the fundamental unit is $\epsilon = \frac{3 + \sqrt{13}}{2}$.
*   Here $u = 1$. We check the conjecture: Does $13$ divide $1$? No ($1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{13}$). The conjecture holds.

In both of these elementary cases, as well as millions of others tested by computers, the prime $p$ fails to divide the coefficient $u$.
