# Generalized Star Height Problem

> **Topic:** Theoretical Computer Science · **ID:** `10-theoretical-cs/generalized-star-height` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

The Generalized Star Height Problem is one of the most enduring open questions in formal language theory and theoretical computer science. It asks whether there exists any regular language whose generalized star height is strictly greater than $1$. 

More precisely, every regular language can be described by a generalized regular expression, which is built from letters of a finite alphabet using the boolean operations (union, intersection, complementation), concatenation, and the Kleene star operation. The generalized star height of a regular language is the minimum nesting depth of Kleene stars required to describe the language using such an expression. 

**The Conjecture:** The generalized star height hierarchy collapses at height 1. That is, for every regular language $L$, the generalized star height of $L$ is at most $1$. Alternatively stated, there is no regular language $L$ requiring a generalized star height of $2$ or more. 

A complete resolution to this problem requires either a constructive proof that every regular language can be represented with a generalized star height of at most $1$, or a counterexample consisting of a specific regular language accompanied by a mathematically rigorous lower-bound proof demonstrating that it cannot be expressed with a star nesting depth of $1$ or less.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

Let $\Sigma$ be a finite alphabet, and let $\Sigma^*$ denote the free monoid generated by $\Sigma$. A language $L$ is a subset of $\Sigma^*$. 

The class of **generalized regular expressions** over $\Sigma$ is defined recursively as follows:
1. $\emptyset$, the empty word $\epsilon$, and $a$ (for every $a \in \Sigma$) are generalized regular expressions.
2. If $E$ and $F$ are generalized regular expressions, then their union $(E \cup F)$, intersection $(E \cap F)$, concatenation $(E \cdot F)$, complementation $(\sim E)$, and Kleene star $(E^*)$ are generalized regular expressions. 
Here, the complementation operator is defined relative to the universal language: $\sim E = \Sigma^* \setminus L(E)$.

The **generalized star height** of a generalized regular expression $E$, denoted $h(E)$, is defined by structural induction:
* $h(\emptyset) = h(\epsilon) = h(a) = 0$ for all $a \in \Sigma$.
* $h(E \cup F) = h(E \cap F) = h(E \cdot F) = \max(h(E), h(F))$.
* $h(\sim E) = h(E)$.
* $h(E^*) = h(E) + 1$.

The generalized star height of a regular language $L$, denoted $gsh(L)$, is defined as the minimum height over all expressions defining $L$:
$$ gsh(L) = \min \{ h(E) \mid L(E) = L \} $$

This is fundamentally different from the **restricted star height** (often just called "star height"), denoted $sh(L)$, which is defined identically but forbids the use of the complementation $(\sim)$ and intersection $(\cap)$ operators in the expressions.

The algebraic foundation of the problem relies on the **syntactic monoid** $M(L)$ of a language $L$. $M(L)$ is defined as the quotient of $\Sigma^*$ by the syntactic congruence $\equiv_L$, where $x \equiv_L y$ if and only if for all $u, v \in \Sigma^*$, $uxv \in L \iff uyv \in L$. A language $L$ is regular if and only if $M(L)$ is finite (Myhill-Nerode Theorem). 

By a seminal theorem of Schützenberger, $gsh(L) = 0$ (the star-free languages) if and only if the syntactic monoid $M(L)$ is finite and **aperiodic** (i.e., group-free, satisfying the identity $x^n = x^{n+1}$ for all $x \in M(L)$ and some integer $n \ge 1$). No such algebraic characterization exists for $gsh(L) \ge 1$.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

The history of star height problems begins with Eggan (1963), who established a relationship between the restricted star height of a language and the cycle rank of non-deterministic finite automata (NFAs) that accept it. Eggan conjectured that the restricted star height hierarchy was infinite. In 1966, Dejean and Schützenberger proved Eggan's conjecture, explicitly demonstrating that for every $k \ge 0$, there exists a language $L_k$ such that $sh(L_k) = k$.

The introduction of complementation drastically alters the landscape. In 1980, Janusz Brzozowski formalized the Generalized Star Height Problem. It was quickly observed that many languages requiring high restricted star height collapse to generalized star height $1$ or $0$ when complementation is permitted. The complementation operator acts as an incredibly powerful "subtractive" tool, capable of bypassing the need to positively construct complex nested loops.

Despite more than four decades of intensive study in automata theory, algebraic language theory, and finite model theory, the state of the art remains remarkably stalled. While the restricted star height problem was shown to be decidable (Hashiguchi in 1988, with a simplified proof using distance automata by Kirsten in 2005), the generalized star height problem remains completely unresolved. It is currently unknown whether $gsh(L)$ can exceed $1$, and further, it is unknown whether the property "$gsh(L) \le k$" is decidable for any $k \ge 1$.

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

While the general conjecture remains open, several major classes of regular languages have been fully characterized or proven to satisfy the conjecture ($gsh(L) \le 1$):

1. **Star-Free Languages ($gsh(L) = 0$):** Fully characterized by Schützenberger (1965). These are exactly the languages definable in First-Order Logic over words, $FO[<]$, and possess an aperiodic syntactic monoid.
2. **Unary Alphabets ($|\Sigma| = 1$):** For any one-letter alphabet, the generalized star height of every regular language is at most $1$. Any unary regular language is a boolean combination of finite sets and arithmetic progressions of the form $(a^n)^*$, which trivially have $gsh \le 1$.
3. **Group Languages:** If the syntactic monoid $M(L)$ is a finite group (rather than a general monoid), it has been proven that $gsh(L) \le 1$. This implies that properties based purely on modular counting (like "the number of $a$'s is congruent to $c \bmod p$") can always be expressed with nesting depth $1$.
4. **Commutative Languages:** Any language over $\Sigma$ whose syntactic monoid is commutative can be constructed as a boolean combination of star-free languages and group languages. Consequently, for all commutative regular languages, $gsh(L) \le 1$.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

The problem remains unsolved due to a profound lack of mathematical invariants for boolean operations on regular languages. 

When evaluating restricted star height, researchers rely on structural induction over automata. The cycle rank of an automaton provides a natural topological barrier. However, complementation breaks this structural link. In an automaton, taking the complement of a language $\sim L$ is structurally trivial: one merely swaps the accepting and rejecting states in the complete Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA) for $L$. This operation completely alters the required algebraic generalized regular expression while keeping the automaton's structural complexity (like cycle rank) perfectly constant. 

Because $\sim E$ can define "all strings that *do not* contain this complex pattern," a language that theoretically requires deeply nested Kleene stars to be built up *positively* can often be defined *negatively* by taking the universal language $\Sigma^*$ (which has $gsh(\Sigma^*) = gsh(\sim \emptyset) = 0$) and subtracting a depth-1 pattern from it. 

Furthermore, standard lower-bound techniques from complexity theory and logic—such as Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games used to prove bounds in first-order logic—fail to easily accommodate the non-local, unbounded nature of the Kleene star operator when interspersed with boolean connectives.

## 6. The Gap

The exact boundary of human knowledge on this problem lies between height $0$ and height $1$. We have a mathematically complete, decidable invariant for $gsh(L) = 0$ (aperiodicity of the syntactic monoid). We have no such necessary and sufficient structural invariant for $gsh(L) = 1$. 

The theoretical barrier is the absence of a "Generalized Star Height Pumping Lemma" or a robust communication complexity theorem that survives complementation. To resolve the conjecture negatively (proving $gsh(L) > 1$ is possible), one must find a structural property $P$ of finite monoids such that any language exhibiting $P$ strictly requires star-nesting depth $2$, and then mathematically prove that no clever application of De Morgan's laws and complementation can bypass this depth. The current mathematical gap is our inability to prove that *any* proposed property $P$ cannot be bypassed by complementation.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

Active research primarily flows through algebraic topology, profinite monoids, and logic.

*   **Profinite Topology:** The most promising modern approach translates the problem into the space of profinite words. The free profinite monoid $\widehat{\Sigma^*}$ is the inverse limit of all finite monoids generated by $\Sigma$. Researchers study the topological closures of languages under generalized star operations.
*   **Decidability via MSO:** Another school of thought works through Monadic Second-Order Logic (MSO). Since Kleene star relates to transitive closure, researchers are analyzing restricted fragments of MSO that might correspond to generalized star height $1$, seeking a decidability proof.
*   **Complexity Approaches:** *(frontier — verify)* Recent working papers have attempted to use two-party communication complexity on the syntactic monoid (specifically analyzing the information bottleneck of evaluating non-solvable monoid products) to establish lower bounds on boolean formulas augmented with star operators, aiming to prove that certain transition semigroups fundamentally require depth-2 evaluations. 
*   **Key Institutions:** IRIF (Paris), University of Warsaw, and researchers aligned with the algebraic theory of automata.

## 8. Future Work

Leading theoreticians suggest the following open pathways:
1.  **Prove Decidability of Height 1:** Before solving the general collapse conjecture, researchers aim to prove whether the predicate "$gsh(L) \le 1$" is decidable. Discovering an algorithm to recognize height-1 languages would likely reveal the algebraic invariants necessary to find a height-2 language.
2.  **Analyze the Full Transformation Semigroup:** Some researchers suspect that if a language with $gsh(L) \ge 2$ exists, its syntactic monoid must be related to the full transformation semigroup $T_n$ for large $n$, which contains complex mixtures of non-solvable groups and highly aperiodic ideal structures.
3.  **Develop Star-Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Games:** Formulating a variant of EF-games that perfectly models generalized regular expressions (where a "Kleene move" allows a player to split a word into an arbitrary number of sub-words) could provide the combinatorial framework needed for a lower bound proof.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

To understand why finding a language with generalized star height $2$ is so difficult, we must look at a concrete case where the restricted star height is $2$, but the generalized star height collapses to $1$ due to the hidden power of complementation.

Let $\Sigma = \{a, b\}$. Consider the language $L_{even-a}$ consisting of all words over $\Sigma$ that contain an *even number* of the letter $a$. 

If we use **restricted regular expressions** (no complementation), we must positively construct every valid sequence. A word with an even number of $a$'s consists of arbitrary sequences of $b$'s, interrupted by pairs of $a$'s (which themselves are separated by arbitrary sequences of $b$'s). The optimal restricted expression is:
$$ E_{restr} = b^* \cdot (a \cdot b^* \cdot a \cdot b^*)^* $$
Because there is a Kleene star on $b^*$ nested strictly inside the outer Kleene star, the restricted star height is exactly $sh(L_{even-a}) = 2$. Furthermore, we know this cannot be $0$ because the syntactic monoid of $L_{even-a}$ is the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is not aperiodic.

Now, we evaluate this using **generalized regular expressions**. We can utilize the complement operator $\sim$ to dramatically reduce the structural depth.
First, we note that $\Sigma^* = \sim \emptyset$. Therefore, the universal language has height $gsh(\Sigma^*) = gsh(\emptyset) = 0$.

Next, how can we express $b^*$ without using a Kleene star? The language $b^*$ is simply "all words that do not contain the letter $a$". We can write this using concatenation and complementation:
$$ E_{no-a} = \sim (\Sigma^* \cdot a \cdot \Sigma^*) $$
Let us calculate the generalized star height of this sub-expression:
$$ gsh(E_{no-a}) = gsh(\sim (\Sigma^* \cdot a \cdot \Sigma^*)) = \max(gsh(\Sigma^*), gsh(a), gsh(\Sigma^*)) = \max(0, 0, 0) = 0 $$
Thus, $b^*$ is a star-free language; it has generalized star height $0$.

Now, we substitute $E_{no-a}$ into our original expression to form a generalized regular expression for the language $L_{even-a}$:
$$ E_{gen} = E_{no-a} \cdot (a \cdot E_{no-a} \cdot a \cdot E_{no-a})^* $$
Let us calculate the height of this new expression. The inner term is $F = a \cdot E_{no-a} \cdot a \cdot E_{no-a}$. 
Since $gsh(E_{no-a}) = 0$ and $gsh(a) = 0$, the concatenation rule gives us $gsh(F) = 0$.
The outer expression applies exactly one Kleene star to $F$:
$$ gsh(F^*) = gsh(F) + 1 = 0 + 1 = 1 $$
Finally, concatenating $E_{no-a}$ (height 0) with $F^*$ (height 1) yields:
$$ gsh(E_{gen}) = \max(0, 1) = 1 $$

By creatively using complementation to "hide" the unbounded iteration of $\Sigma^*$ at depth $0$, a language that intrinsically requires nested loops in a positive sense is cleanly reduced to generalized star height $1$. This collapsing behavior applies to a vast array of complex languages, which is why the generalized star height conjecture continues to defy resolution.
