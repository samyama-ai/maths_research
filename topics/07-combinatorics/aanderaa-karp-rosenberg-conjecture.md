# Aanderaa-Karp-Rosenberg Conjecture (Evasiveness of Monotone Graph Properties)

> **Topic:** Combinatorics & Graph Theory · **ID:** `07-combinatorics/aanderaa-karp-rosenberg-conjecture` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

Let $P$ be a property of simple undirected graphs on the labelled vertex set $[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$. An algorithm learns the input graph only by asking questions of the form "is $\{i,j\}$ an edge?" There are $N=\binom{n}{2}$ possible questions.

**Conjecture (Karp; "evasiveness conjecture").** Every graph property that is
- *nontrivial* (some graph on $[n]$ has it, some does not), and
- *monotone* (adding edges never destroys the property), and
- *isomorphism-invariant* (depends only on the isomorphism class)

requires all $\binom{n}{2}$ queries in the worst case: $D(P)=\binom{n}{2}$.

A proof must handle every $n$ and every such $P$. A disproof needs one explicit $n$, one nontrivial monotone isomorphism-invariant $P$, and a decision tree that always stops after at most $\binom{n}{2}-1$ queries.

Two companion statements are usually bundled with it. The **Aanderaa–Rosenberg conjecture** (the original, weaker form) asserts $D(P)=\Omega(n^2)$ — this is **proved**. The **randomized AKR conjecture** (Karp) asserts $R(P)=\Omega(n^2)$ for the two-sided-error randomized query complexity — this is **open**, and is the reason the entry as a whole is far from closed.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

Identify a graph on $[n]$ with its indicator vector $x\in\{0,1\}^{E}$, $E=\binom{[n]}{2}$, so $P$ is a Boolean function $f_P:\{0,1\}^N\to\{0,1\}$.

**Decision tree complexity.** A deterministic decision tree $T$ is a rooted binary tree whose internal nodes are labelled by variables $e\in E$ and whose leaves are labelled $0/1$. $D(f)=\min_T \operatorname{depth}(T)$ over trees computing $f$. Always $D(f)\le N$; $f$ is **evasive** iff $D(f)=N$.

**Symmetry.** The symmetric group $S_n$ acts on $E$ by $\sigma\cdot\{i,j\}=\{\sigma i,\sigma j\}$; isomorphism-invariance means $f_P(x^\sigma)=f_P(x)$. The induced group is transitive on $E$, and contains for every $n$ the subgroup generated by an $n$-cycle.

**Simplicial complex.** Monotonicity lets one encode the non-instances as a downward-closed set system
$$\Delta_P=\{\,x\in\{0,1\}^{E} : f_P(x)=0\,\}\subseteq 2^{E},$$
an abstract simplicial complex on ground set $E$ (faces = graphs lacking $P$). $S_n$ acts simplicially on $\Delta_P$.

**Key lemma (Kahn–Saks–Sturtevant 1984).** If a monotone $f$ is **non**-evasive then $\Delta_f$ is *collapsible*, hence contractible, hence $\mathbb{Z}$-acyclic:
$$\tilde H_i(\Delta_f;\mathbb{Z})=0 \ \ \forall i, \qquad \text{in particular}\quad \tilde\chi(\Delta_f)=\sum_{F\in\Delta_f}(-1)^{\dim F}=-1+\sum_{\emptyset\neq F}(-1)^{|F|-1}=0 .$$
(The proof is induction on $N$: querying $e$ splits $\Delta$ into $\mathrm{del}_e\Delta$ and $\mathrm{lk}_e\Delta$, and non-evasiveness makes the deletion collapse onto the link.)

**Oliver's fixed-point theorem (1975).** Let $\Gamma$ be a finite group possessing a normal chain $\Gamma_1\trianglelefteq\Gamma_2\trianglelefteq\Gamma$ with $\Gamma_1$ a $p$-group, $\Gamma_2/\Gamma_1$ cyclic, $\Gamma/\Gamma_2$ a $q$-group ($p,q$ primes, possibly $1$). If $\Gamma$ acts simplicially on a finite $\mathbb{Z}$-acyclic complex $\Delta$, then the fixed subcomplex $\Delta^{\Gamma}$ is nonempty (indeed $\mathbb{Z}$-acyclic).

Combining: if $P$ is nonevasive, pick such a $\Gamma\le S_n$ acting on $E$; then $\Delta_P$ has a $\Gamma$-fixed face, i.e. a $\Gamma$-invariant graph lacking $P$. Choosing $\Gamma$ so that its only invariant graphs are $K_n$ and the empty graph contradicts nontriviality. This is the whole engine of the known cases.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

- **1973.** A. Rosenberg poses the problem in *SIGACT News*, reporting S. Aanderaa's observation and conjecturing $D(P)=\Omega(n^2)$ for monotone properties; he noted the $n^2$ claim fails without isomorphism-invariance.
- **1974.** Best, van Emde Boas and Lenstra (Math. Centrum report) sharpen the conjecture to full evasiveness and verify small $n$; Karp independently promotes the evasiveness form, whence "Aanderaa–Karp–Rosenberg".
- **1976.** Rivest and Vuillemin prove the Aanderaa–Rosenberg conjecture: $D(P)\ge n^2/16$, using a counting/prime-power argument.
- **1984.** Kahn, Saks and Sturtevant introduce the topological method, proving evasiveness for all prime powers $n=p^k$ and raising the general bound to $n^2/4-o(n^2)$.
- **1988.** Yao proves the bipartite analogue in full.
- **1991–2007.** Randomized side: Yao's $\Omega(n\log^{1/12}n)$, King's $\Omega(n^{5/4})$, Hajnal's $\Omega(n^{4/3})$, and Chakrabarti–Khot's $\Omega(n^{4/3}\log^{1/3}n)$.
- **2010–2013.** Korneffel–Triesch push the deterministic constant to $\tfrac{8}{25}n^2+o(n^2)$; Scheidweiler–Triesch to $\left(\tfrac13-o(1)\right)n^2$. Babai, Banerjee, Kulkarni and Naik connect evasiveness to prime gaps; Kulkarni reformulates via circuit/"weak evasiveness" equivalences.

Current SOTA: **proved** $D(P)\ge(1/3-o(1))n^2$ for all $n$ and $D(P)=\binom n2$ exactly when $n$ is a prime power; **open** for general $n$ and open with a polynomial gap in the randomized model.

## 5. Principal Obstacles

- **The group runs out.** Oliver's theorem needs a group $\Gamma$ that is (almost) solvable in a very restricted layered sense *and* whose only invariant graphs on $[n]$ are trivial. For $n=p^k$ one uses the affine group $x\mapsto ax+b$ over $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}$, which is edge-transitive and has exactly the required normal chain. For $n$ with two distinct prime factors (already $n=6$) no subgroup of $S_n$ is known that satisfies both requirements simultaneously — the topological method has no input.
- **Acyclicity is all you get.** Non-evasiveness yields collapsibility, a strictly stronger property than acyclicity, but no invariant beyond homology/fixed points has been made to work. Euler-characteristic arguments alone are too weak: for many properties $\tilde\chi(\Delta_P)=0$ while $\Delta_P$ is not contractible.
- **A hard counterexample nearby.** The natural generalisation is *false*: Illies (1978) exhibited a nontrivial monotone Boolean function on 12 variables, invariant under a transitive group, that is non-evasive. So any proof must use graph-specific structure, not transitivity alone — this kills all purely group-theoretic routes.
- **Randomized model has no topology.** Collapsibility arguments say nothing about expected query cost. Lower bounds there come from adversary/packing arguments (Hajnal's, Chakrabarti–Khot's), which saturate around $n^{4/3}$ because the hard "witness" structures they build have $\Theta(n^{4/3})$ scale.
- **No candidate counterexample.** Extensive search over monotone properties for small $n$ has produced nothing non-evasive, so there is no structural hint of what a disproof would look like.

## 6. The Gap

Two precise gaps:

1. **Deterministic.** Proven: $\left(\tfrac13-o(1)\right)n^2 \le D(P)$; target: $D(P)=\tfrac12 n^2-\tfrac12 n$. The missing step is a *fixed-point theorem for non-prime-power $n$*: either extend Oliver's conclusion to groups outside the $p$-group/cyclic/$q$-group chain when the complex is collapsible (not merely acyclic), or find a non-group route from collapsibility of $\Delta_P$ to a contradiction with $S_n$-invariance. Equivalently (Kulkarni), one may settle **weak evasiveness**, $D(P)\ge(1-o(1))\binom n2$ for all $n$, which for graph properties is known to be essentially as strong as evasiveness itself.
2. **Randomized.** Proven: $R(P)=\Omega(n^{4/3}\log^{1/3}n)$; conjectured $\Omega(n^2)$; no monotone graph property is known with $R(P)=o(n^2)$. Closing a gap of a factor $n^{2/3}$ needs a lower-bound technique (adversary weights, polynomial degree, or noise-sensitivity) sensitive to isomorphism-invariance, which none of the current ones is.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

- **Topological combinatorics.** Continued attempts to replace Oliver's theorem with fixed-point statements tailored to collapsible complexes and to non-solvable group actions; the sharpest formulations of the programme are in Miller's monograph and in Kulkarni's circuit-lens reformulation.
- **Number-theoretic route.** The Babai–Banerjee–Kulkarni–Naik strategy — deduce evasiveness at $n$ from a prime power close to $n$ — improves automatically with progress on prime gaps; unconditionally it already covers a density-1 set, and stronger gap results (Cramér-type) would cover all large $n$ for weak evasiveness. *(frontier — verify: any claim of full coverage under RH.)*
- **Structured classes.** Evasiveness for $k$-uniform hypergraph properties, for forbidden-subgraph and minor-closed properties, and for properties of bounded description complexity.
- **Randomized side.** Query-complexity groups (in TCS: Rutgers, Dartmouth, TIFR, IIT/CMI, Weizmann) study whether Fourier/noise-sensitivity methods — following O'Donnell–Saks–Schramm–Servedio's $R(f)\ge \Omega(s(f)^{?})$-type results and the sensitivity theorem of Huang (2019) — can be made to exploit edge-transitivity.

## 8. Future Work

- Prove weak evasiveness for all $n$; by the graph-property equivalence this essentially settles the deterministic conjecture.
- Develop an "Oliver theorem for collapsible complexes": characterise which finite groups must fix a point when acting on a collapsible complex. This is a clean, self-contained question in equivariant topology.
- Find a proof that avoids groups altogether — e.g. a monotone-Boolean-function argument that uses the specific lattice of graph isomorphism classes, so as to dodge the Illies counterexample.
- On the randomized side, either exhibit a monotone graph property with $R(P)=o(n^2)$ (which would be a spectacular algorithmic result) or design an adversary argument whose weight function is $S_n$-equivariant.
- Settle the *directed* and *hypergraph* analogues, where the group-theoretic input differs and may be easier.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

Take $n=3$, $E=\{a,b,c\}$ with $a=\{1,2\}$, $b=\{1,3\}$, $c=\{2,3\}$, $N=\binom32=3$, and $P=$ "the graph is connected" (nontrivial, monotone, isomorphism-invariant).

**Adversary argument.** The adversary answers "non-edge" to the first two queries, say $a$ and $b$. The remaining consistent graphs are $\{c\}$ (a single edge, disconnected) and $\emptyset$ (disconnected) — wait, both are disconnected, so instead answer "edge" to the first query and "non-edge" to the second: after $a=1,b=0$ the surviving graphs are $\{a\}$ (disconnected) and $\{a,c\}$ (the path $2\!-\!1$, $2\!-\!3$: connected). The two disagree on $P$, so the third query is forced. Hence $D(P)=3=\binom32$: **evasive**.

**Topological cross-check.** The non-instances are the disconnected graphs on $3$ vertices: $\emptyset,\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}$ — every $2$-edge graph on $3$ vertices is a path, hence connected. So
$$\Delta_P=\{\emptyset,\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}\},$$
three isolated points. Its reduced Euler characteristic is
$$\tilde\chi(\Delta_P)=\\#\text{vertices}-1=3-1=2\neq 0,$$
and $\tilde H_0(\Delta_P;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^2\neq 0$, so $\Delta_P$ is not $\mathbb{Z}$-acyclic. By the KSS lemma a nonevasive monotone function has collapsible, hence acyclic, $\Delta$; therefore $P$ is evasive — matching the adversary computation.

**How the group enters.** $n=3$ is a prime power, so the KSS machine applies directly: take $\Gamma=\mathrm{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_3)=\{x\mapsto ax+b: a\in\mathbb{F}_3^\times,b\in\mathbb{F}_3\}$, of order $6$, with normal chain $1\trianglelefteq C_3\trianglelefteq \Gamma$ ($C_3$ a $3$-group, $\Gamma/C_3\cong C_2$ a $2$-group) — exactly Oliver's hypothesis. $\Gamma$ is transitive on $E$, so its only invariant graphs are $\emptyset$ and $K_3$. If $P$ were nonevasive, $\Delta_P$ would be acyclic and would contain a $\Gamma$-fixed face, i.e. $\emptyset$ or $K_3$ would be a *non*-instance together with all of its orbit — forcing $P$ trivial. Contradiction.

**Where it breaks at $n=6$.** Repeating the argument needs a subgroup $\Gamma\le S_6$ that is edge-transitive on the $15$ pairs and has a $p$-group/cyclic/$q$-group normal chain. Edge-transitive subgroups of $S_6$ (e.g. $\mathrm{PGL}(2,5)\cong S_5$ acting on $6$ points) are not of that shape, and the solvable ones are not edge-transitive. That single missing group is the whole open problem in miniature.
