# Erdős-Moser Problem

> **Topic:** Combinatorics & Graph Theory · **ID:** `07-combinatorics/erdos-moser-problem` · **Status:** solved-recently

## 1. Problem Statement / Conjecture

Let $A=\{a_1<a_2<\dots<a_n\}$ be a set of $n$ **distinct** real numbers and let $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Define the *concentration count*

$$f(A,x)\;=\;\\#\Big\{S\subseteq A:\ \textstyle\sum_{a\in S}a=x\Big\},\qquad f(n)=\max_{|A|=n,\,x\in\mathbb{R}} f(A,x).$$

**Erdős–Moser problem (1965).** Determine the growth of $f(n)$. Erdős and Moser conjectured

$$f(n)\;=\;O\!\left(\frac{2^n}{n^{3/2}}\right),$$

i.e. that *distinctness* of the $a_i$ improves the classical Littlewood–Offord–Erdős bound $f(n)\le\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\Theta(2^n n^{-1/2})$ by a full factor of $n$.

**Sharp form.** Identify the extremal configuration: is the maximum attained by the symmetric arithmetic progression $A_n^\ast=\{-(n-1),-(n-3),\dots,n-3,n-1\}$ with $x=0$?

A complete solution requires (i) the order-of-magnitude upper bound, matched by the lower bound from $A_n^\ast$, and (ii) the exact inequality $f(A,x)\le f(A_n^\ast,0)$ for every $n$-element $A$ and every $x$. Both are now theorems; the surviving open content is the vector-valued and stability versions in §6.

*Disambiguation.* Two other problems carry this name: the **Erdős–Moser equation** $1^k+2^k+\cdots+(m-1)^k=m^k$ (open; only $1+2=3$ known), and the **Erdős–Moser tournament problem** on transitive subtournaments. Both are treated briefly in §3.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

**Signed formulation.** Writing $\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\}$ for membership, $f(A,x)=\\#\{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^n:\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_i=x\}$. Equivalently, with $\eta_i\in\{-1,+1\}$,

$$f(A,x)=\\#\Big\{\eta\in\{\pm1\}^n:\ \sum_i \eta_i a_i = 2x-\textstyle\sum_i a_i\Big\},$$

so $2^{-n}f(A,x)=\Pr[\sum_i\eta_i a_i=t]$ for i.i.d. Rademacher $\eta_i$: this is a **small-ball / anticoncentration** quantity.

**Littlewood–Offord–Erdős theorem (1945).** If $|a_i|\ge 1$ for all $i$, then $\Pr[\sum\eta_i a_i \in I]\le \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}2^{-n}$ for any open interval $I$ of length $2$; the proof is Sperner's theorem applied to the antichain of sign patterns.

**Graded-poset formulation.** For $A=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$, the $k$-subsets ordered by componentwise domination form the poset $L(k,n-k)$, whose rank generating function is the **Gaussian binomial coefficient**

$$\binom{n}{k}_q=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1-q^{n-k+i}}{1-q^{i}}=\sum_{s}\\#\{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}:\ \Sigma S-\tbinom{k+1}{2}=s\}\,q^{s}.$$

Centering $\{1,\dots,n\}$ to $A_n^\ast$ makes all ranks share the same centre, so

$$f(A_n^\ast,0)\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Big[q^{\,k(n-k)/2}\Big]\binom{n}{k}_q \quad(\text{terms with }k(n-k)\text{ odd contribute }0).$$

**Unimodality.** Sylvester's theorem — the coefficient sequence of $\binom{n}{k}_q$ is symmetric and unimodal — is the algebraic engine; Stanley's proof derives it from the **hard Lefschetz theorem** applied to the cohomology of the Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)$ with the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-action generated by multiplication by the hyperplane class.

**Asymptotics.** For $A_n^\ast$, $\operatorname{Var}(\sum\varepsilon_i b_i)=\tfrac14\sum_i b_i^2\sim n^3/12$ with $b_i=2i-n-1$; a local central limit theorem on the lattice $2\mathbb{Z}$ gives

$$f(A_n^\ast,0)\;\sim\;2\sqrt{6/\pi}\;\frac{2^n}{n^{3/2}}\approx 2.764\cdot 2^n n^{-3/2}.$$

**Halász's higher-order refinement.** If $R_k(A)$ denotes the number of solutions of $a_{i_1}+\cdots+a_{i_k}=a_{j_1}+\cdots+a_{j_k}$, then $\sup_x f(A,x)\ll_k 2^n n^{-(2k+1)/2}$ provided $R_k(A)$ is of the minimal order; $k=1$ recovers the Erdős–Moser exponent $3/2$.

## 3. History & State of the Art (SOTA)

- **1943–45.** Littlewood and Offord bound the concentration of random signed sums at $O(2^n n^{-1/2}\log n)$; Erdős removes the log via Sperner, obtaining the sharp $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ for $|a_i|\ge1$.
- **1965.** Erdős, in *Extremal problems in number theory*, records the Erdős–Moser question: distinctness should buy an extra factor $n$. Erdős and Moser themselves prove $O(2^n n^{-3/2}\log n)$.
- **1965.** **Sárközy and Szemerédi** prove the conjectured order $f(n)=O(2^n n^{-3/2})$, by a counting/graph argument on near-equal subset sums. The constant they obtain is far from optimal.
- **1970.** Kleitman extends Littlewood–Offord to vectors of norm $\ge1$ in any normed space, obtaining the same $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ bound.
- **1977.** Halász introduces Fourier-analytic estimates giving $n^{-(2k+1)/2}$ decay under additive-energy hypotheses — the first method sensitive to arithmetic structure rather than order structure.
- **1980.** **Stanley** proves the exact conjecture: $f(A,x)\le f(A_n^\ast,0)$, using Weyl-group representation theory and the hard Lefschetz theorem; this simultaneously yields the Sperner property for $L(k,n-k)$.
- **1982.** **Proctor** replaces the algebraic geometry with an elementary $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ linear-algebra argument, making the proof self-contained.
- **1988.** Frankl and Füredi solve the high-dimensional Littlewood–Offord problem for vectors of norm $\ge1$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$.
- **2009–2021.** Tao–Vu and Nguyen–Vu develop *inverse* Littlewood–Offord theory: strong anticoncentration fails only when $A$ is close to a generalized arithmetic progression. Ferber–Jain–Luh–Samotij (2021) give sharp counting versions.

**Other problems named Erdős–Moser.** (i) The Diophantine equation $\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}i^k=m^k$: Gallot, Moree and Zudilin (2011) show any solution with $k\ge2$ forces $m>10^{10^9}$ — still open. (ii) Erdős–Moser (1964) on tournaments: every tournament on $n$ vertices contains a transitive subtournament on $\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor+1$ vertices, and the truth lies between $(1+o(1))\log_2 n$ and $2\log_2 n$ — the constant is open.

## 4. Partial Results / Verified Cases

| Regime | Result |
|---|---|
| $|a_i|\ge1$, reals | $f\le\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ (Erdős 1945), sharp for $a_i\equiv1$ |
| $a_i$ distinct reals, order of magnitude | $f(n)=\Theta(2^n n^{-3/2})$ (Sárközy–Szemerédi 1965) |
| $a_i$ distinct reals, exact | $f(n)=f(A_n^\ast,0)$, all $n\ge1$ (Stanley 1980; Proctor 1982) |
| Fixed cardinality $|S|=k$ | Coefficients of $\binom{n}{k}_q$ unimodal (Sylvester; Stanley) |
| Vectors in a normed space, $\|v_i\|\ge1$ | $f\le\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ (Kleitman 1970) |
| Distinct vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$, $d\ge2$ | $O(2^n n^{-3/2})$ known only for $d=1$-reducible configurations; general $d$ open |
| $A$ Sidon / minimal additive energy | $O(2^n n^{-5/2})$ and beyond (Halász 1977) |
| Small $n$ | $f(1)=1$, $f(2)=2$, $f(3)=2$, $f(4)=4$, $f(5)=6$, $f(6)=10$ — direct enumeration |
| Finite groups | Optimal Littlewood–Offord inequalities in abelian groups (Juškevičius–Šemetulskis 2019) |

## 5. Principal Obstacles

The *original* problem is closed; the obstacles concern its natural extensions.

- **Sperner-type methods are order-theoretic and one-dimensional.** Erdős's proof works because $\{S:\Sigma S=x\}$ is an antichain in the Boolean lattice once the $a_i$ are positive. Distinctness gives a *finer* grading (by sum, not just size) that Sperner cannot see; recovering it needs unimodality of $\binom{n}{k}_q$, which has no known bijective or injective proof. Every known proof is linear-algebraic ($\mathfrak{sl}_2$, hard Lefschetz, or Lindström–Gessel–Viennot style flows), and these break the moment the ground set is not totally ordered.
- **Loss of a linear order in $\mathbb{R}^d$.** For distinct vectors there is no Gaussian-binomial model: the extremal configuration is not conjecturally unique, and hard Lefschetz has no analogue for the relevant partially ordered structure.
- **Fourier methods lose logarithms and constants.** Halász's method controls $|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|=\prod_i|\cos(\pi a_i\xi)|$ and needs quantitative separation of the $a_i$; distinctness of reals gives no such quantitative separation, so the method cannot be run at the exact-constant level.
- **Stability is not extractable.** Both algebraic proofs are inequalities between dimensions of eigenspaces; they give no information about which $A$ come close to the maximum, so "almost-extremal $\Rightarrow$ almost-arithmetic-progression" statements need entirely different (inverse-theoretic) machinery.

## 6. The Gap

Proven: for distinct **reals**, $\max_x f(A,x)\le f(A_n^\ast,0)\sim 2\sqrt{6/\pi}\,2^n n^{-3/2}$, with equality only for translates/dilates of $A_n^\ast$. The gap is threefold.

1. **Dimension.** For $n$ distinct vectors $v_1,\dots,v_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ ($d\ge2$), is $\max_{x}\\#\{S:\sum_{i\in S}v_i=x\}=O(2^n n^{-3/2})$? Distinctness in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is much weaker than in $\mathbb{R}$ — a set can be distinct yet lie on few parallel lines — and no order-theoretic reduction is known.
2. **Higher exponents.** Halász's $n^{-(2k+1)/2}$ bounds require hypotheses on $k$-fold additive energy. The clean combinatorial statement "if all subset sums of size $\le k$ are distinct then $f=O(2^n n^{-(2k+1)/2})$" with the *sharp* constant is unproved for $k\ge2$.
3. **Stability.** No characterization of sets with $f(A,x)\ge(1-\varepsilon)f(n)$ exists. Crossing this requires an inverse theorem at the exact-extremal scale, whereas current inverse Littlewood–Offord results operate at the $2^n n^{-C}$ scale with unspecified constants.

## 7. Current Research (as of June 2026)

- **Inverse and counting Littlewood–Offord theory** (Vu and collaborators at Yale; Ferber at UC Irvine; Jain; Samotij at Tel Aviv; Kwan, Sah and Sawhney) — sharp counting of coefficient vectors with prescribed concentration, and applications to random-matrix singularity. This is the most active descendant of the Erdős–Moser question.
- **Group-theoretic Littlewood–Offord.** Juškevičius and coauthors (Vilnius) obtain optimal inequalities in abelian and some non-abelian groups; the distinct-element ("Erdős–Moser") analogue in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and in $\mathbb{Z}^d$ is under active study. *(frontier — verify)*
- **Algebraic unimodality.** Continued search for a combinatorial (injective) proof of unimodality of $\binom{n}{k}_q$, which would give the first non-Lefschetz proof of Stanley's theorem and plausibly generalize to $\mathbb{R}^d$. *(frontier — verify)*
- **Anticoncentration for polynomials and subgraph counts**, where the Erdős–Moser exponent $3/2$ is the degree-1 base case of a conjectural hierarchy.

## 8. Future Work

- Prove or refute the $\mathbb{R}^d$ distinct-vector conjecture, starting with $d=2$ and configurations in general position.
- Develop a stability version: show $f(A,x)\ge(1-\varepsilon)f(n)$ forces $A$ to be within $o(n)$ elements of an arithmetic progression.
- Find an elementary (Sperner- or flow-based) proof of unimodality of Gaussian binomials, per Stanley's own repeated call in his log-concavity survey.
- Sharpen Halász's constants to obtain exact extremal statements at exponent $5/2$ for Sidon sets.
- Transfer the exact bound to finite abelian groups, where the extremal set should be an arithmetic progression modulo $p$.

## 10. Worked Example / Concrete Special Case

**Case $n=4$.**

*Naive set.* Take $A=\{1,2,3,4\}$. Subset sums range over $0..10$; the counts are the coefficients of $\prod_{j=1}^{4}(1+q^{j})=1+q+q^2+2q^3+2q^4+2q^5+2q^6+2q^7+q^8+q^9+q^{10}$. The maximum is $2$ (e.g. $x=5$: $\{1,4\},\{2,3\}$).

*Extremal set.* Take the centered progression $A_4^\ast=\{-3,-1,1,3\}$ and $x=0$. The solutions are
$$\varnothing,\quad\{-1,1\},\quad\{-3,3\},\quad\{-3,-1,1,3\},$$
so $f(A_4^\ast,0)=4$. Under the shift $a\mapsto a+\tfrac52$ these correspond exactly to subsets $S\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}$ with $\Sigma S=\tfrac52|S|$: the empty set ($k=0$), $\{1,4\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ ($k=2$, sum $5$), and the full set ($k=4$, sum $10$). That is $1+2+1$, i.e. the central coefficients of $\binom{4}{0}_q,\binom{4}{2}_q,\binom{4}{4}_q$ — precisely the formula of §2. Centering aligns the centres of all four Gaussian binomials at one value of $x$; the uncentered set $\{1,2,3,4\}$ cannot do this, which is why it only reaches $2$.

*Comparison of bounds.* Erdős's Sperner bound gives $\binom{4}{2}=6$; Stanley's theorem gives the truth, $4$. Asymptotically the two differ by the factor $n$ at the heart of the conjecture: at $n=20$, $\binom{20}{10}=184{,}756$ while $f(20)=f(A_{20}^\ast,0)=17{,}678$ — a ratio of about $10.5\approx n/2$.

*Why distinctness is essential.* For $A=\{1,1,1,1\}$ (not a set) the count at $x=2$ is $\binom42=6$, matching the Sperner bound. Any repetition destroys the $n^{-3/2}$ decay, which is exactly the content of the Erdős–Moser hypothesis.
